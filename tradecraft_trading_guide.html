01 / Foundations

What trading actually is

Trading means buying and selling a financial asset with a plan. A good trade is not a guess; it is a decision with defined risk.

Stocks

A share represents a small ownership stake in a company. Prices move as buyers and sellers react to earnings, expectations, news, and broader conditions.

ETFs

Exchange-traded funds hold a basket of assets. They can provide broader diversification than a single company stock.

Market vs. limit orders

A market order prioritizes execution at the best available price. A limit order sets the maximum buy price or minimum sell price you will accept; execution is not guaranteed.

Time frames

Day trading opens and closes positions in one day. Swing trading may hold for days or weeks. Investing typically focuses on longer-term ownership.

02 / Reading charts

Understand the price story

Charts summarize past price and volume. They do not predict the future.

Candlesticks

Each candle shows open, high, low, and close for a selected period. The body shows the open-to-close range; the wicks show the extremes.

Bullish candle: close above open. Bearish candle: close below open.

Trend

An uptrend often forms higher highs and higher lows. A downtrend often forms lower highs and lower lows. Sideways markets move within a range.

Support & resistance

Support is an area where buying has previously appeared. Resistance is an area where selling has previously appeared. Neither level is guaranteed to hold.

Volume

Volume measures how many shares traded. A price move with unusually high volume can indicate stronger participation, but it is not proof of direction.

Chart rule: mark levels and write down what would prove your idea wrong before entering a trade.
03 / Build a repeatable plan

A simple practice framework

Use this as a learning checklist in a simulator. It is not a guaranteed or recommended trading strategy.

Choose one liquid market to study

Start by observing a broad-market ETF or a widely traded stock. Learn its usual volatility, trading hours, and major scheduled events.

Define the setup

Write objective conditions: the trend you want, the price area that matters, and what confirmation you need. Avoid vague rules such as “it looks ready.”

Set entry, stop, and target first

Decide where the idea becomes invalid and where you might take profit. If you cannot define the risk before entry, skip the trade.

Record the outcome

Log the date, reason for entry, planned risk, result, screenshot, and whether you followed your rules. Review a meaningful sample, not one lucky trade.

Practice before using real money

Use paper trading to learn order types and test whether you can follow a plan. Simulated fills and emotions differ from live trading.

04 / Protect your downside

Risk management comes first

Important: You can lose some or all of the money you put into a trade. Day trading is especially risky. Never trade borrowed money, money needed for essentials, or money you cannot afford to lose.

Position sizing

Size a position from the amount you are prepared to lose if your exit is reached — not from the amount you hope to make.

Stops are not guarantees

A stop order can execute at a worse price during gaps or fast markets. Slippage and fees affect results.

Avoid leverage

Margin, options, and leveraged products can magnify losses and add complexity. Beginners should understand the risks before considering them.

Protect your mindset

Do not chase losses, revenge trade, or increase size to “make it back.” A planned no-trade day is a valid decision.
